Understanding Overconfidence

Every Friday, I write a to-do list for the weekend. It's a mix of errands (many Amazon returns), chores around the house, and work (studiously getting ahead for the next week). Every Friday, the list is ambitious, but I am confident I can get it done. Every Sunday, inevitably, half the list has gone unchecked, rolling over to the next weekend where I will surely repeat the same exercise. If you took our Overconfidence Quiz above and were told you are overconfident, congratulations, you are like most other people in this world, including this author.

Research shows that despite repeated feedback in the form of seeing outcomes we are confident about not materializing, humans tend to still be overconfident in their judgments. Even experts, who are supposed to be well-calibrated in their judgments, are biased to be overconfident, including doctors making clinical judgments (Croskerry & Norman, 2008), traders making investment decisions (Deaves et al., 2010), and CEOs guiding corporate strategy (Fischhoff et al., 1977).

Researchers have found that, just like most of our quiz takers, people are often wrong about information that they believe they know the answer to (Griffin et al., 1990). Overconfidence does not only occur when answering esoteric trivia questions, where baseline confidence is low. Even when people think they are 100% sure they know a piece of information, their actual accuracy is closer to 70%.

Ok, so we are overconfident in our knowledge judgments? Why does that matter? I can just ChatGPT the correct answer. Well, it turns out people are not just overconfident in their judgments about informational knowledge. We're also overconfident when it comes to making judgments about others and ourselves. Research by Griffin, Dunning, and Ross (Griffin & Tversky, 1992) paints an example, where a group of research subjects were asked to read the below description of a student, Peter, and then assess Peter in terms of how opinionated, outgoing, and domineering they thought him to be:

Peter is a sophomore at Stanford. He is 19 years old and is thinking of majoring in international relations. When asked if he was willing to participate in a discussion on the topic of abortion with four other people, Peter agreed to do so. The discussion was scheduled to last one hour. It ended up lasting one hour and fifteen minutes. Out of that time Peter talked for 45 minutes (the next most talkative person talked 18 minutes).

Subjects were highly likely to use Peter's monopolization of the conversation to draw inferences about his personality traits, despite the limited context about the discussion. However, asking the subjects to consider the different ways the discussion could have looked (e.g., was Peter asked to be the discussion leader, was no one else wanting to speak?), enabled people to make less extreme trait judgments, reducing their confidence that the monopolization of the one conversation was a complete representation of Peter.

It's often the case that we take small interactions with people, and use them to make confident judgments about who the person is and what the person is like, without considering all of the other factors surrounding the situation that could make our judgments inaccurate. A new coworker comes to Friday morning's meeting without the document he promised to deliver, and we judge him as lazy, thinking he spent Thursday evening at the bar watching football, rather than working. We don't think about all of the other circumstances which could have caused the situation (maybe his child was sick and he spent the evening in urgent care).

Researchers suggests this pattern of using small pieces of information to make confident judgments is because people have a bias to focus on the strength of evidence (e.g., Bob did not do the work he promised to do, indicative of laziness), without considering the weight of the evidence (e.g., this is just one missed deliverable and we don't know the context behind why he didn't complete the work) (Malmendier & Tate, 2005). Therefore, even when making judgments about trivia facts, we focus on information we know and do not consider the breadth of information we do not know, that could make our judgment inaccurate.

Ok, so humans are overconfident. Is overconfidence irrational?

It depends on what we mean by "irrational". If by rational we mean exactly right about how share we are, then yes, most of us fall short. Our confidence runs hotter than our accuracy. That's the classic "heuristics and biases": our minds lean on quick cues and vivid narratives. A single striking fact can overpower the quieter math of base rates and uncertainty. In this sense, overconfidence is a systematic error.

But there is another, softer meaning of rational that matters in real life. If rational means doing pretty well with limited time, attention, and memory, then a little overconfidence can actually be adaptive. Calibrating every belief to perfection is slow and expensive. We usually don't have the luxury to pause, collect more data, and compute the ideal range before we act - say, when buying lunch at a food truck.. From a "ressource-rational" view, your brain is trying to make a good bet under constraints: pick the beef taco that feels right and keep the line moving. Sometimes acting quickly with a bit of extra conviction pays off and we can adjust if needed later - try your friend's shrimp taco next time if it turns out better.

This also fits the "dual-process" picture. The fast mode is fluent and confident—it says, "This beef taco looks sooo good," and moves on. The slow mode widens our confidence intervals and makes us consider alternatives—"Maybe the shrimp taco is better"—but it costs effort and time (you might even buy both and deal with leftovers). Most of life runs in the fast lane, which is why the same confidence patterns repeat. We only shift into the slow gear when the situation really demands it.

So where does that leave us? Overconfidence is imperfect: it doesn't track the truth as precisely as it claims. But it can be instrumentally useful: it helps us start projects, make decisions and coordinate under limits on resources. The trick is matching the mindset to the moment. When the stakes are high and errors are costly (medical decisions, big financial moves), we should slow down, check base rates, and deliberately widen our ranges. When stakes are modest or decisions are reversible, a little extra confidence can be the engine that gets us moving.

Closing notes

If you made this far, you already did the hardest part: you paused long enough to notice your own uncertainty. That's the whole game!

If you want a quick mental habit to try, before locking in a judgement, ask yourself: What's one plausible alternative story? Maybe Peter talked more because he was asked to lead. Maybe your coworker missed the document because their dog was sick. Naming just one alternative makes a difference: it cools the heat of a single cue, and it nudges your confidence toward a size that reality can actually fit inside.

In the end, a rational mind isn't one that never errs; it's the one that learns. Keep the engine of confidence, add a few brakes, and you'll make bets in a world that rarely hands us perfect information. Have fun!
