Understanding Overconfidence (placeholder)

Every Friday, I write a to-do list for the weekend. It's a mix of errands (many Amazon returns), chores around the house, and work (studiously getting ahead for the next week). Every Friday, the list is ambitious, but I am confident I can get it done. Every Sunday, inevitably, half the list has gone unchecked, rolling over to the next weekend where I will surely repeat the same exercise. If you took our Overconfidence Quiz and were told you are overconfident, congratulations, you are like most other people in this world, including this author.

Research shows that despite repeated feedback in the form of seeing outcomes we are confident about not materializing, humans tend to still be overconfident in their judgments. Even experts, who are supposed to be well-calibrated in their judgments, are biased to be overconfident (including doctors making clinical judgments, traders making investment decisions, and CEOs guiding corporate strategy).

Researchers have found that, just like most of our quiz takers, people are often wrong about information that they believe they know the answer to. Overconfidence does not only occur when answering esoteric trivia questions, where baseline confidence is low. Even when people think they are 100% sure they know a piece of information, their actual accuracy is closer to 70%.
